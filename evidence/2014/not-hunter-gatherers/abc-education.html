Bruce Pascoe: Aboriginal Agriculture, Technology and Ingenuity

ABC Education

Posted Wed 7 Dec 2022 at 11:13amWed 7 Dec 2022 at 11:13amWed 7 Dec 2022 at 11:13am, updated Mon 8 May 2023 at 10:18amMon 8 May 2023 at 10:18amMon 8 May 2023 at 10:18am

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander viewers are advised that this resource contains images of people who have died.

In 2014, Bruce Pascoe wrote a book called Dark Emu that challenged the belief that the First Australians were hunter-gatherers.

SUBJECTS

Geography, History, Science, Technologies

YEARS

5–6, 7–8, 9–10

In researching his book, Bruce examined the journals of the early explorers and found evidence of a complex civilisation that was using sophisticated technologies to live, farm and manage the land.

By looking at scientific research, archival footage and the journals of early explorers, we learn about the vast agricultural fields, ingenious aquaculture systems, sophisticated use of fire and successful industries that existed in Australia prior to colonisation.

Notes for the audience

  • Since 2019, Pascoe's work has been evaluated differently by some people, who don't agree with his interpretations of historical sources. This resource contains excerpts from the original texts and scientific evidence that Bruce draws on. We encourage you to evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of all historical sources.

  • The most significant academic study of Pascoe's work and methodology has been conducted by eminent Australian anthropologist Professor Peter Sutton and respected field archaeologist Dr Keryn Walshe in their book Farmers or Hunter Gatherers? The Dark Emu Debate (Melbourne University Press, 2021).

1. Introducing Bruce Pascoe

Bruce Pascoe is a Yuin, Bunurong and Tasmanian man.

He is an award-winning writer who has worked as a teacher, a farmer and an Aboriginal-language researcher. Learn how Bruce's investigation into his own family background led to his discovery of Australia's long agricultural history prior to colonisation.

2. Stone tools

Bruce Pascoe takes us for a walk on Yuin Country, around his farm in far-east Gippsland.

We learn what some of the stones in our natural environment can reveal about Australia's past. We also hear about the different uses for stone tools and how stone was imported from other locations.

3. The explorers' diaries

In their journals, some early explorers of Australia described scenes of large-scale agriculture by Aboriginal people.

Sir Thomas Mitchell rode past 9 miles of harvested grain. Sir George Grey saw miles of deeply tilled gardens used for growing vegetables. Bruce Pascoe explores the reasons he thinks why Aboriginal civilisation, economy, agriculture and aquaculture are rarely mentioned in history books.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander viewers are advised that this resource contains images of people who have died.

4. Grain and bread

Bruce Pascoe states that Aboriginal people were the world's first bakers and that they invented bread.

Learn about the archaeological evidence for ancient seed grinding in Australia. Bruce introduces us to a map that shows Aboriginal people were harvesting grain and grinding it into flour in the Australian desert. We also learn about perennial Australian plants adapted to our climate and capable of surviving drought.

4.1 World's first bakers?

When did humans begin grinding seeds to make flour?

Many people believe bread-making began in Egypt or Mesopotamia as long as 17,000 years ago. Archaeologists have recently found evidence that Indigenous Australians were producing flour 65,000 years ago.

Were they the world's first bakers?

  2. 2.Why was Pascoe surprised when he learned that Aboriginal people ground flour 35,000 years ago?
  3. 3.What evidence did archaeologists find in 2017 to show that Aboriginal people were the world's first bakers?

4.3 Baking today

Bruce Pascoe introduces us to a multipurpose stone that was used by the "Old People" for grinding, crushing and hammering.

It remains an important tool in the production of bread using native grasses like panicum and kangaroo grass.

Pascoe explains that native grasses can be grown sustainably and abundantly, and they're good for us!

5. Roast duck and cake in the desert

In his 1849 journal "Narrative of an Expedition into Central Australia", British explorer Charles Sturt writes about being saved by a large group of Aboriginal people in the desert.

He and his party were given water, roasted ducks and cake and provided with a new hut to sleep in.

Bruce Pascoe uses excerpts from Sturt's journal to provide insight into the prosperous lives of the Aboriginal people who lived in Australia's arid interior.

7. Murnong and tubers

In his 1841 journals, George Augustus Robinson wrote that he saw Aboriginal women harvesting murnong (the yam daisy) across a plain in Victoria "as far as the eye could see".

Murnong is a root vegetable (or tuber) that grows in the south-east of Australia, and it was a staple food for many Aboriginal people. Early explorers recorded observations of large murnong farms. Bruce shows us how murnong is grown.

7.1 Murnong

In the 1830s, Sir Thomas Mitchell saw murnong (the yam daisy) growing to the horizon as he rode near the Grampians in Victoria.

In this video, we hear how murnong fields were destroyed by sheep. The consequence for Indigenous Australians was catastrophic.

7.2 Murnong seeds and storage

How do we grow and care for a plant that was a crucial part of the diet of Aboriginal peoples prior to the arrival of Europeans?

Pascoe shows us some murnong seeds in his garage, shares some growing tips and encourages students to try growing it.

Pascoe notes that the plant is "pretty robust" and probably stores well for future use.

9. Aquaculture

The ingenuity of Aboriginal people in aquaculture can still be seen by visiting the fish traps in Brewarrina, NSW, and the UNESCO World Heritage-listed eel traps at Lake Condah, Victoria.

The early explorers' journals also give us a great insight into aquaculture technologies used by Aboriginal people.

9.1 Lake Condah aquaculture

In this video, we learn about the network of channels and ponds engineered by the Gunditjmara people to farm eels at Lake Condah. We find out about the way the Gunditjmara people preserved the eels and about the industry this created.

Loading...

9.2 Aquaculture machine

Bruce Pascoe describes an ingenious way Aboriginal people caught fish without nets, spears or fishing line.

Designed by Aboriginal people along the Murray River, the fishing machine tossed fish from the river onto the bank. All the operator had to do was reset the machine after each catch. Alas, its efficiency provided one English observer with an opportunity to demonstrate how "racism and prejudice can twist your brain into a knot".

11. Housing

In this chapter, we learn about the remains of large villages of stone houses that can still be seen at Lake Condah.

We hear from the early explorers who describe coming across villages of comfortable and well-designed homes.

Aboriginal people were using stone, timber, bark, rope and other technologies to build houses designed for the varying conditions across the country.

12. Birds and totems

We take a walk on Country and learn how birds can signify a change in the seasons.

Bruce introduces us to totems and tells us about the importance of black duck to Yuin people. Totems are part of a complex spiritual system interwoven into some Aboriginal cultures. A totem can be a plant or an animal inherited by a language group or family. Those people then have a responsibility to care for their totems.

13. Fire

Aboriginal people used fire for farming, to manage land and to keep tracks open in the bush.

Sir Thomas Mitchell's 1848 journal notes that fire was used in farming and to create open forests, and that both people and wildlife were dependent on this. Bruce says that wildfires were not as common as they are today due to the way Aboriginal people used fire. He believes the history of fire in Australia can be read by examining the stumps of old trees.
