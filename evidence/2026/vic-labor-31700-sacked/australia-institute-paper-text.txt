Victorian Opposition
Fiscal Strategy: An
Assessment
This paper assesses the Victorian Opposition's fiscal
strategy, which aims for a surplus by 2032 and ignores
Victoria's underlying fiscal health. It finds the
centrepiece measure—$22 billion in savings from a
public sector "back office" hiring freeze—unachievable.
Such savings would require elimination of around 31,700
jobs, much more than back office staff, and could push
unemployment toward 5.4%. The Opposition’s cost-of-
living spending proposals are largely uncosted and
would chiefly benefit higher-income households.

Summary
The fiscal strategy outlined by Victorian Opposition Leader Jess Wilson is based on returning
the state budget to cash surplus by 2032 while claiming to deliver cost-of-living relief and
reducing debt. This paper analyses the size of that task and the options for achieving it.

The Victorian budget is currently projected by Treasury to run cash deficits of between $7
and $8 billion annually over the forward estimates. These deficits are driven primarily by
capital investment rather than recurrent spending. Importantly, the government's operating
activities — its ongoing revenues and expenditures — are projected to remain in surplus,
and Victoria's net worth is forecast to grow from around $183 billion to $216 billion by
2029-30.

Importantly, the cash deficit position of the Victorian Government that has become an
object of concern for many political analysts should not be of concern to any financial
analysts familiar with the cash flow statements and balance sheets of any large corporation
that was borrowing to fund the long-lived assets required to drive future growth.

This paper finds a key Opposition savings measure to be either unsubstantiated or
unachievable, namely: The claim of saving $22 billion through a public sector back office
hiring freeze is found to be implausible. Apart from announcing “a hiring freeze on back
office roles” there is no further detail from the Victorian Opposition but achieving that level
of savings would require eliminating around 31,700 positions by 2036-37. That would be
over half the entire Victorian Public Service headcount of 58,169 (excluding statutory
authorities, corporations, schools, hospitals) and is well beyond any reasonable definition of
"back office roles."

The macroeconomic consequences of such public sector job shedding could be an increase
in Victoria’s unemployment rate to 5.4%, up from a projected baseline of 4.75% and in turn
a significant reduction in consumer spending with flow on impacts to Victorian businesses.

Many of the remaining proposals from the Victorian Opposition — including payroll tax
relief for private schools and GPs, stamp duty exemptions for first home buyers, and land
tax relief — are either too vague to cost reliably and/or are unlikely to deliver meaningful
cost-of-living benefits to ordinary households. Energy policy proposals involve reverting to
fossil fuel dependence without substantive engagement with energy security challenges.

The analysis concludes that the Opposition's path to surplus relies overwhelmingly on
measures that cannot deliver the savings claimed. The fiscal strategy as presented contains
a substantial "black hole," and the contractionary effect of genuinely achieving a surplus
which would require withdrawing approximately $8 billion annually from the Victorian
economy.

Introduction
The purpose of this paper is to examine the Victorian Opposition fiscal strategy as outlined
by the Leader of the Opposition, Jess Wilson.1 Her plan is to:

    •   Return the budget to cash surplus2 by 2032

    •   Reduce the debt and interest burden

    •   Deliver cost of living relief

    •   Make Victoria the best place to do business

    •   Drive investment and jobs

    •   Payroll Tax relief

    •   Exempt first home buyers from Stamp Duty up to $1 million

    •   Land Tax relief

    •   Unlock housing supply and reform planning

    •   Restore energy security and reduce costs

    •   Shift infrastructure to local priorities

    •   Hiring freeze on back office roles to guarantee essential services

Before going through this plan in detail, it is worth noting immediately that any program
that might achieve the first two dot points would necessarily involve very large cuts to
government programs, cuts with significant impact on consumer spending and
unemployment. But before evaluating the specific proposals the paper first provides an
overview of fiscal indicators to give an overall summary of Victoria’s fiscal position.

It is important to highlight that while standard private sector accounting does not treat large
expenditures on long lived assets (such as private hospitals, private toll roads or private
schools) as an ‘expense’ to be deducted from that year’s revenue, combining recurrent
expenditure (eg wages) and infrastructure investment (eg building a hospital) has become
common when state and federal government budgets are being described. To be clear,
while no person who bought a house, spending more than they earned in the same year,
would be described as “living beyond their means”, that is exactly how state governments
spending large amounts of money on infrastructure are commonly described in Australia.

Leaving aside the confusion that comes with the conflation of recurrent and capital
expenditure, what is clear from the Victorian Budget Papers is that if the Victorian
Opposition was to achieve their goal of delivering a ‘cash surplus’ by 2032 they will need to
reduce annual expenditure (either recurrent or capital) by around $8 billion per year.

Table 1 shows expected total receipts of $115,619 million in 2026-27, that includes taxes
worth $43,888 million, grants of $56,365 million and other revenues of $11,366 million.

The next item in Table 1 is payments of $109,142 million which are recorded as a negative
because they represent an outflow of money.

The next line item is “Net cash flows from operating activities” from the budget papers and
which is obtained by subtracting all payments from receipts. This is the outcome which
reflects ordinary on-going spending and revenue on the part of government and excludes
investments in long-lived assets such as infrastructure. This operating result can be either in
surplus or deficit. If in deficit it would be shown as a negative number but in the table the
figures for this item are all positive indicating that the net cash flows from operating
activities are all surpluses. The surplus figures here mean the Victorian government covers
ordinary government spending out of its revenue.

On the following line in Table 1 we have “Net cash flows from investments in non-financial
assets” this is basically the Victorian government’s investment in roads, buildings such as
schools and hospitals, bridges and a host of other capital works. Planned expenditure under
this category is $14,181 million in 2026-27.

MAKE VICTORIA THE BEST PLACE TO DO BUSINESS
The Opposition’s fiscal strategy document says it wants to make Victoria the best place to
do business. It complains about a lack of confidence and says the fiscal position is out of
control. Bizarrely, while the Victorian Opposition talks of the need to boost confidence in
the state, it is the Opposition that is (knowingly or unwittingly) talking down the fiscal
position of the state rather than talking up Victoria’s positive attributes.

Melbourne always seems to do well in terms of global rankings among cities in the world
and in 2026 topped Time Out’s ranking.9 In the 2025 Oxford Economics Global Cities Index,
Melbourne scored 6th, beaten by just New York, London, Paris, San Jose and Seattle. The
Oxford rankings are based on economics, human capital, quality of life, environment, and
governance.10 The most recent, the Global Liveability Index published by the Economist
Intelligence Unit had Melbourne third after Copenhagen and Vienna and outscoring other
Australian cities.11

These rankings suggest Victoria is already doing many things right. Invest Victoria
emphasises other rankings that put Melbourne at number 1 in Australia for liveability and
4th in the world.12

This item is reminiscent of the federal Opposition campaign in 2025 in which they
anticipated cutting 40,000 public servants based in Canberra.13 That policy showed signs of

modelling is contained in an appendix to this paper.

Conclusion
The Victorian Opposition’s fiscal statement is an old-fashioned policy document saying they
can spend on big promises funded through the magic pudding of cutting waste together
with eliminating some programs for Indigenous people. The Opposition has decided to align
itself with those who would doom Victoria with an austerity program rather than try to
enhance the wellbeing of Victorians and address their needs.

The Victorian Opposition's fiscal strategy sets austerity targets that the evidence suggests
cannot be met through the measures proposed. The centrepiece savings claim —$22 billion
from a public sector hiring freeze —seems to account for the overwhelming majority of the
cuts required for the Opposition to reach surplus by 2036-37, yet this item does not
withstand scrutiny. This measure would require eliminating over half the entire core public
service in Victoria, with significant consequences for employment and service delivery
across the state.

The remaining proposals are either too vaguely specified to be costed or are unlikely to
generate savings of the scale required. Several measures framed as cost-of-living relief
would in practice deliver the greatest benefits to higher-income households and
corporations rather than ordinary Victorians. Despite that they are likely to be expensive if
ever implemented.

More fundamentally, the goal of returning the budget to cash surplus by 2032 reflects a
misunderstanding of Victoria's fiscal position. The state's operating accounts are already in
surplus; the deficit arises from capital investment that is growing the state's net worth and
productive capacity. Pursuing a cash surplus would require withdrawing billions of dollars
from the Victorian economy each year at a time when public investment is supporting
growth, employment and essential services. It would also deny valuable infrastructure to
the people of Victoria.

In short, the Opposition's fiscal plan as currently presented is not credible. The savings are
illusory, the costs are real, and the underlying economic rationale is flawed.

To be clear, the aim of producing a surplus and reducing debt in the current environment
would require cuts to government spending of at least $8 billion per annum. Those cuts
reduce the income of the private sector and so reduce jobs in the private sector. On top of
that, if the Opposition plans to cut staff costs by $22 billion could be achieved, that alone
would cut employment in Victoria by 31,700 people.
