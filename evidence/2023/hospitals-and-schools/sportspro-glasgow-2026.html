How Glasgow 2026 plans to save the Commonwealth Games

Victoria’s withdrawal as host three years ago didn’t just threaten the 2026 Commonwealth Games, it jeopardised its very existence. Commonwealth Sport chief executive Katie Sadleir explains how a leaner, more efficient hosting model will futureproof the event for decades to come.
22 July 2026 Josh Sim
Features

Getty Images

SHARE THIS ARTICLE

The Commonwealth Games are nothing if not adaptable.

They began life as the British Empire Games back in 1930 when Britain sought to strengthen cultural ties with its colonies as its formal control weakened.

However, decolonisation was not the death knell for the movement that it should have been and the Commonwealth instead became a global platform for members who might be crowded out in the United Nations or the International Olympic Committee (IOC).

The Games grew in scale and stature and by the turn of the century, the Commonwealth Games were the second biggest multi-sport event in the world. Ambitious governments in India and Malaysia used the Games to make a mark on the world stage, Australia sought to showcase its sporting infrastructure, and Manchester saw an opportunity to both celebrate Queen Elizabeth II’s Golden Jubilee and revitalise the east end of the city.

The Glasgow 2014 games were seen as a high watermark in terms of organisation, attendance and public interest, driving local investment, tourism and sporting success for the host nation.

Scottish swimmer Duncan Scott lends his support to the Commonwealth Games (Image credit: Glasgow 2026)

But rising costs and expanded scale limited the number of cities who were willing and able to play host and the Games’ sporting legitimacy struggled with the absence of so many major nations.

Gold Coast 2018 was well received but criticised as an expensive vanity project and plans to bring the Games to Africa for the first time were plunged into disarray by Durban’s withdrawal.

Luckily, Birmingham was ready to step in to rave reviews.

But if to lose one host is misfortune then to lose two is carelessness, and that’s exactly what happened when the Australian state of Victoria handed back the rights to stage the 2026 Games three years ago.

Victoria’s decision was financial. Despite plans for a more efficient, decentralised Games, costs ballooned to exceed initial estimates by around AUS$5 billion (US$3.46 billion).

This time there was no obvious replacement waiting in the wings and the Commonwealth Games were facing an existential crisis.

The short- and long-term pressures seemed insurmountable – staging an event in 2026 seemed impossible and the future of the movement seemed bleak.

Victoria as a catalyst

Yet it survived. Glasgow emerged as the host of a new, scaled down Games which Commonwealth Sport – previously the Commonwealth Games Federation (CGF) – hopes will serve as a sustainable footprint that safeguards the future of the movement.

Katie Sadleir, chief executive of Commonwealth Sport, doesn’t deny the disarray the organisation was in when Victoria withdrew. But she tells SportsPro that it had long recognised the need for greater sustainability long before Victoria pulled the plug.

“The government of Australia gave me seven hours notice that Victoria was pulling out,” says Sadleir.

“It was far from ideal, but it led to a movement to look at our product, what we’re doing, and how we did need to reset and reimagine the Games for the future.

“So, [Victoria’s departure] was not planned, but it certainly has put us in a much stronger position in terms of where we are at the moment.”

She adds: “Before Victoria pulled out, we knew we needed to reset the Games. We were committed to transform the Games to a size and scale that our members outside of Australia and the UK could host.

“We brought forward that thinking earlier because of the Victoria withdrawal, but it was definitely part of what we want to be over the next 100 years, so we can take the Commonwealth Games around the Commonwealth. The changes that we made as a result of this have really excited our members in terms of being potential hosts.”

Glasgow 2026 will be the first test of the redesigned model. The programme has been reduced to ten sports, while organisers will rely almost entirely on existing venues rather than new infrastructure – some of which were constructed for the previous Games in the city 12 years ago.

Commonwealth Sport is targeting a 60 per cent reduction in delivery costs. Glasgow 2026 is expected to cost around UK£160 million (US$214.6 million), compared with approximately UK£800 million (US$1.07 billion) for Birmingham 2022. Most of this funding will come from a UK£100 million (US$133.7 million) settlement paid by Victoria as a result of its withdrawal.

“Glasgow 2026 actually took more costs out, and they did that by making sensible financial decisions,” Sadleir says. “They are using existing facilities, a smaller footprint, and sustainability measures that mean we won’t spend lots of money on cars and transport. The athletes will live close to where they will compete, and in a much more compact space.”

The challenge is to ensure that a model that prioritises sustainability over scale can win over audiences, sponsors and broadcasters.

Changing broadcast priorities

Indeed, there will be a significant shift in how the Games are broadcast in the UK. The BBC, which has aired every single iteration since 1954, was outbid by pay-TV broadcaster TNT Sports. For the first time since television coverage began 72 years ago, the Games will sit behind a paywall.

Some observers has suggested that blanket free-to-air (FTA) coverage on the BBC was essential to the appeal of the Commonwealth Games. Others have questioned whether the Commonwealth Games can work as a pay-TV proposition and that the BBC’s reported offer was influenced by the pared-back nature of the Games and its relative appeal to British audiences.

The only FTA coverage of the Games will be a daily highlights show on Channel 5 – as mandated by the UK government’s ‘crown jewels’ list of protected events.

However, both Commonwealth Sport and TNT Sports’ parent company Warner. Bros Discovery (WBD) are adamant that the event can be a major draw, with the broadcaster promising to overhaul coverage of the event using its streaming and storytelling capabilities.

WBD has promised to use its experience of covering the Olympics to elevate the Commonwealth Games through more than 600 hours of live coverage, a National Football League (NFL) Redzone-style whiparound show, and a dedicated linear channel. The Games will be available with a standard HBO Max subscription costing UK£4.99 – a price point that both sides believe will ensure wide availability.

“We believe we can create the most immersive coverage of a Commonwealth Games ever covered… we can put the games into the hands of the viewer and the customer, and let them experience what they want, when they want, where they want,” says Scott Young, executive vice president at WBD Sports Europe.

“The reach of HBO Max in the UK is far greater than I think most people probably comprehend.”

Commonwealth Sport is confident that expanded coverage, coupled with a greater digital presence will ensure both dedicated and casual sports fans are served as well as possible and present the Games in the best possible light.

“WBD… recognised the broadcasting changes that were going on [in the market] and wanted to bring some of them [the changes] to life,” adds Sadleir.

“We’ve moved from 300 hours of Games coverage to 600 hours of coverage … so that is an extensive opportunity to see far more of the Games than people have ever been able to see before.

“The world is just changing in terms of how people are looking at sport. They’re looking at it on phones, they’re streaming it – it’s a different world in terms of how we’re consuming it. What we will see during this Games is a variety of different broadcasting approaches.”

Away from the UK, broadcast agreements are up 30 per cent from Birmingham 2022, with Commonwealth Sport securing deals with Seven Network in Australia, CBC in Canada and Sky and TV3 in New Zealand. There has also been demand from outside the Commonwealth, with BeIN Sports securing the US rights.

TNT Sports are putting significant resources into the Commonwealth Games (Image credit: TNT Sports)

Stronger commercial relationships

Commonwealth Sport has also been keen to ensure a smaller event has the same sponsorship appeal, so it has engineered a strategic shift away from relationships tied solely to the main event towards year-round partnerships.

Sadleir says discussions with long-standing partners have remained positive, while Ahmedabad’s selection as host of the 2030 Games has encouraged interest in longer-term agreements.

“We have some really loyal partners for multiple Games, which are still there when you don’t even know where the future of the Games is going to be,” Sadleir says. “Longines is one who’s been with us for a very long period.

“Having India for the 2030 Games has been a really attractive opportunity for some of our partners to be thinking about as we go forward.”

That confidence is reflected in Glasgow 2026’s commercial programme. Major brands including Bupa, BYD and Coca-Cola have joined as partners, providing some vindication of the approach.

Coca-Cola is one of several blue chip brands to back Glasgow 2026 (Image credit: Glasgow 2026)

The race to host the next Commonwealth Games

Looking even further ahead and the picture is considerably brighter than it was three years ago. There was a genuine bidding race for 2030, with Ahmedabad fending off interest from Nigeria and Canada. Sadleir confirms Nigeria remains interested, while New Zealand has repeatedly expressed interest in hosting the 2034 Games and is expected to send a delegation to Glasgow.

“Nigeria is definitely keen to stay, and they are meeting with us in Glasgow,” Sadleir says.

“New Zealand is another country that has put up its hand twice for 2034, so they have been relentless. I met with their minister of sport when I was down there recently, and they talked about how the Commonwealth Games runs in their blood.

“So those are the countries that have been public, but there are other countries that at this stage would prefer to be private.”

Sadleir argues this is a direct result of Glasgow’s low-cost hosting model and Commonwealth Sport will share lessons from the event to prospective hosts in a bid to replicate any cost savings.

Glasgow 2026 will be a much more humble celebration of sport than Glasgow 2014 but, if it can replicate just a fraction of that success, it could save an entire sporting movement.

Not a bad legacy at all.
